5.1 Upper Console Wells vs. Dedicated CHANNEL INSPECTOR View#

Cave Recorder provides two complementary workspaces for viewing and shaping the processors on any Input Track or Subgroup Bus:

  1. The Four Upper Console Wells (MIX CONSOLE View, CMD/CTRL + 1): Docked permanently along the upper deck of the Mix Console so you can adjust inserts, EQ curves, and compression while keeping all channel faders and bus meters in view.
  2. The Dedicated CHANNEL INSPECTOR View (CMD/CTRL + 3): Expands the selected channel or subgroup across the entire screen with a high-resolution Dual-Trace Real-Time Spectrum Analyzer, dedicated rotary parameter knobs, multi-bus output routing popovers, and a scrolling Gain Reduction history trace.

5.1.1 The Four Upper Console Wells (MIX CONSOLE)#

Clicking any Input Track header (CH01..CH18) or clicking the SEL button on any Subgroup Bus (DRUM BUS, INST BUS) immediately binds the four Upper Console Wells to that strip:

5.1.2 Full-Screen CHANNEL INSPECTOR View (CMD/CTRL + 3)#

Pressing CMD + 3 (macOS) or CTRL + 3 (Windows/Linux) switches to the dedicated CHANNEL INSPECTOR view. Along the top selector bar, single-click pills (01 Kick In, 02 Snare Top, 03 Overhead L, 04 Guitar Amp, DRUM BUS, INST BUS) let you step across every Input Track and Subgroup Bus in the session without returning to the Mix Console.

In the CHANNEL INSPECTOR view, the left column anchors a full-height channel strip (complete with active Pass selector * PASS 01 on Input Tracks or STEREO SUBGROUP / SUBGROUP BUS badges on Subgroups, Output Bus selector, M, S, REC, MON, Ø, Φ, TRIM, rotary PAN, full-scale calibrated meter with +12 dB to -48 dB markings, and CLR CLIP), while the main workspace expands:

  • EQ & DUAL-TRACE 4096-PT RTA: Adds direct band-select buttons for all 12 bands (B1 through B12), all seven filter shape buttons (BELL, LSHF, HSHF, HPF, LPF, NTCH, TILT), and dedicated precision rotary knobs on the right (FREQ, GAIN, Q).
  • VCA COMPRESSOR & GR TRACE: Adds a continuous scrolling Gain Reduction (GR Trace) timeline, extended ratio and parallel blend buttons (SC HPF, LINK, 2:1, 4:1, 8:1, 20:1, MIX 100%, MIX 50%), and six dedicated rotary knobs on the right (THRESH, RATIO, ATTACK, RELEASE, MAKEUP, MIX).

  • Dedicated Inspector Keyboard Shortcuts: While inspecting any strip, press H to toggle the compressor Sidechain High-Pass Filter (SC HPF), X to clear the channel peak overload latch (CLR CLIP), E to expand or collapse Pass Lanes (PASSES), or SHIFT + M / S / A / I / P to toggle Mute, Solo, Record Arm, Input Monitor, or Polarity (Ø).

5.1.3 Reordering the 8-Slot Rack, SWAP EQ/CMP & RESET#

In Well 1 (01 Kick In or DRUM BUS), the eight processing slots (1 through 8) execute sequentially from Slot 1 down to Slot 8:

  • Clicking a Slot Pill (SLOT N PROCESSOR Overlay): Clicking any slot pill (1 through 8) in Upper Well 1 or in the left column of CHANNEL INSPECTOR opens the floating SLOT N PROCESSOR selector menu. From this menu you can assign any of Cave Recorder's built-in zero-latency studio processors (12-Band Dynamic EQ, VCA Compressor, Tape Saturation, Vocal De-Esser, Context-Aware Gate, Signal Repair, Vocal Rider, or Aux Send Tap), clear the slot via Empty Slot (---), or choose CLAP / AU Plugin to expand the Indexed Plugin Catalog Subpanel (INDEXED PLUGIN CATALOG (CLAP & AU READY)), load any indexed third-party .clap plugin or native macOS Audio Unit (AUv3 / AUv2) effect, and open its floating editor window with PLUGIN GUI (detailed in Section 5.7.3).
  • Dragging :: or Clicking ^ / v: Reorders any processor (1:CLAP, 2:12B EQ, 3:VCA CMP, 4:AUX SND) to an earlier or later slot in real time without audio clicks or dropouts. Moving 4:AUX SND above 3:VCA CMP, for example, taps the channel's Aux Sends before compression even when the send is set to rack-tap mode.
  • SWAP EQ/CMP Button: Instant one-click macro in the top-left rack header that swaps the slot positions of the 12B EQ and VCA CMP processors. Use SWAP EQ/CMP to audition whether filtering low-end mud before the compressor detector (EQ -> CMP) or shaping tonal balance after dynamic peak control (CMP -> EQ) sounds more natural on a vocal, bass, or drum bus.
  • B (Slot Bypass) & RESET: Clicking B next to any slot (or on the 12B EQ / VCA CMP badge) bypasses that single processor in place (illuminated amber). Clicking the crimson RESET button returns all built-in processors on the selected strip to their flat factory defaults.

5.2.4 Differential Envelope TransientShaper#

Unlike compressors that depend on an absolute decibel threshold (and therefore react differently to quiet ghost notes versus loud rimshots), the built-in TransientShaper uses a level-independent differential envelope detector. It compares a fast envelope follower (E_fast, 1.0 ms attack / 25.0 ms release) against a slow envelope follower (E_slow, 25.0 ms attack / 150.0 ms release) in the logarithmic decibel domain:

Δ_dB(n) = 20 log10(E_fast(n) + ε) - 20 log10(E_slow(n) + ε)

Because the difference Δ_dB(n) depends only on the rate of change of the envelope and not its absolute volume, every ghost note and loud accent receives consistent transient shaping:

  • Attack (-12.0 dB to +12.0 dB): Positive values amplify the initial 1 ms to 20 ms stick crack, kick beater click, or acoustic guitar pick attack. Negative values soften overly spiky transients or push close mics deeper into the room.
  • Sustain (-24.0 dB to +24.0 dB): Positive values bring up the decaying drum shell ring, room ambience, or guitar chord bloom after the initial attack has passed. Negative values tighten boomy rehearsal rooms and shorten long tom or kick tails cleanly without hard gate chatter.
  • Detector Focus Band (20 Hz to 16,000 Hz): Focuses the differential detector on a specific frequency zone (for example, 2.5 kHz to trigger only on snare wire snap while ignoring low-frequency kick bleed).
  • Soft Clip Output Guard: Engages a zero-overshoot tanh saturator on the output stage so boosting Attack by +8 dB adds visceral drum impact without clipping downstream buses.

5.3.2 Operating Interactive EQ Nodes & Reading the Dual-Trace RTA#

You can shape EQ either via the right-hand rotary knobs (FREQ, GAIN, Q) in the CHANNEL INSPECTOR view (CMD/CTRL + 3) or by manipulating the colored band nodes directly inside the EQ & RTA graph on both the Mix Console and Channel Inspector:

  • Click + Drag Node Horizontally / Vertically: Sweeps FREQ (20 Hz to 20 kHz) and GAIN (-24.0 dB to +24.0 dB) simultaneously.
  • Mouse Scroll Wheel (or SHIFT + Vertical Drag) Over a Node: Narrows or widens the filter Q (0.10 to 18.00).
  • SHIFT + Drag Node: Engages 10x fine precision (0.1 dB gain steps and sub-Hz frequency resolution) for locking onto exact room resonances.
  • Double-Click Node (or OPT/ALT + Click): Resets the band's GAIN to 0.0 dB.

Behind the white composite EQ curve, the Dual-Trace High-Resolution Real-Time Spectrum Analyzer (4096-PT RTA) renders two live acoustic traces simultaneously:

  1. Pre-EQ Input Spectrum (Darker Shaded Trace): Shows the raw frequency energy entering the 12B EQ slot.
  2. Post-EQ Output Spectrum (Bright Emerald Trace): Shows the resulting frequency energy leaving the 12B EQ slot after all active bands are applied.

Because the high-resolution analyzer resolves narrow intervals down into the lowest bass octaves (20 Hz to 250 Hz), you can visually distinguish between the 55 Hz fundamental of a kick drum and the 82 Hz low E string of a bass guitar, place a narrow BELL or NTCH node directly on a ringing 480 Hz snare overtone spike, and watch the emerald post-EQ trace flatten out in real time. On any record-armed track (R), the Dual-Trace RTA remains active even when software Input Monitoring (I) is turned off, allowing you to inspect live microphone spectrum and dial in high-pass or notch filters during zero-latency hardware direct-monitoring sessions.

5.3.3 Per-Band Dynamic EQ (Threshold-Driven Resonance & Mud Control)#

In a live rehearsal or tracking room, a problematic frequency is rarely constant. A rhythm guitar cabinet might sound balanced during open chords but boom violently at 165 Hz only on chugged palm-mutes. A lead vocalist might sound warm in the verse but turn piercing at 3.2 kHz only when belting the chorus. Cutting 165 Hz or 3.2 kHz by -6 dB with a static EQ fixes the loud peaks at the cost of leaving the instrument thin and lifeless for the rest of the song.

To solve this, any of the 12 parametric bands (B1 through B12) in 12B EQ can be switched into Dynamic EQ Mode:

5.3.4 VocalRider: Automatic Zero-Latency Vocal Leveler with Breath & Bleed Freeze Gate#

When a singer performs live in the same room as a loud band, their distance from the microphone capsule naturally shifts between intimate verses and full-voice choruses. Feeding those 12 dB level swings directly into a compressor forces the compressor into heavy -12 dB gain reduction on loud notes while leaving quiet lines buried. Worse, if you try to use a standard automatic gain rider in a live rehearsal room, the rider mistakes the pause between vocal lines for a "quiet vocal" and cranks the fader up by +6 dB, flooding the mix with loud snare and cymbal bleed.

Cave Recorder's VocalRider is engineered specifically for live-room vocals, bass, and solo instruments:

  • Target Level (-36.0 dBFS to -6.0 dBFS, default -18.0 dBFS) & Range (±6.0 dB): Smoothly rides the pre-compressor channel level by up to +6.0 dB on quiet phrases and -6.0 dB on loud phrases with zero lookahead latency, delivering a consistent -18 dBFS vocal into your downstream compressor.
  • Speech-Weighted Sidechain Detector (250 Hz to 4.0 kHz): Filters out low-frequency kick/bass thump below 250 Hz and hi-hat sizzle above 4.0 kHz so the gain rider reacts strictly to fundamental vocal body and presence.
  • Breath & Bleed Freeze Gate (Freeze Threshold, -70.0 dBFS to -24.0 dBFS, default -48.0 dBFS): Whenever the speech-weighted vocal energy drops below Freeze Threshold (such as when the singer steps back or takes a breath between phrases), the rider immediately freezes its gain fader (or glides gently toward 0.0 dB unity) so live drum and amplifier room spill is never boosted into the mix.

5.3.5 Split-Band DeEsser & Dynamic Low-Shelf Plosive Arrestor#

Close vocal microphones in rehearsal and live tracking rooms suffer from two distinct transient faults at opposite ends of the spectrum: low-frequency P and B breath blasts (plosives) that thump the subwoofers, and piercing high-frequency S, Sh, and T sibilance (4.5 kHz to 9.0 kHz) that turns harsh when pushed through vocal saturation or plate reverb.

The built-in DeEsser solves both problems in a single zero-latency processor:

  1. Stage 1: Dynamic Low-Shelf Plosive Arrestor (120 Hz): An 80 Hz low-pass transient detector monitors sub-bass air blasts. Normal vocal chest resonance (110 Hz to 250 Hz) passes through completely untouched, but the instant a P or B microphone pop hits below 80 Hz, a fast (0.5 ms attack, 60 ms release) dynamic low-shelf filter at 120 Hz attenuates the low end by up to -18.0 dB, removing the thump without thinning out the singer's voice.
  2. Stage 2: Split-Band Sibilance Suppressor (2.0 kHz to 16.0 kHz, default 6.5 kHz, up to -24.0 dB): Unlike wideband de-essers that duck the entire vocal whenever an S occurs (causing an unnatural "lisp"), the split-band detector isolates only the sibilant frequency band (Sibilance Freq adjustable from 2.0 kHz to 16.0 kHz with default 6.5 kHz, Max Reduction 0.0 dB to -24.0 dB with default -8.0 dB) and compresses only the high-frequency shelf while leaving the 200 Hz to 3.0 kHz vocal vowel core at 100% volume. Engaging SC LISTEN solos the isolated sibilance detector so you can sweep Sibilance Freq to the exact whistle pitch of the singer's S consonants.

5.3.6 Haas-Free Stereo MicroPitchDoubler#

A classic studio trick for widening a single mono rhythm guitar, synthesizer lead, or backing vocal is the "Haas delay" (delaying the right channel by 15 ms relative to the left). Unfortunately, summing a static Haas delay to mono (MONO M⊕) creates severe comb-filtering notches that make the instrument sound hollow and phasey on single-speaker PA systems or phones.

The MicroPitchDoubler generates lush stereo width that remains 100% solid when folded to mono:

  • Opposite-Polarity Dual Pitch Shifters (Detune: 1.0 to 30.0 cents, default ±9.0 cents): Pitch-shifts the Left wet voice slightly downward (-Detune cents) and the Right wet voice slightly upward (+Detune cents) using dual overlapping Hann-windowed grain buffers driven by a 0.75 Hz quadrature LFO (90° stereo phase offset).
  • Decorrelated Pre-Delay (5.0 ms to 40.0 ms, default 14.0 ms L / 19.0 ms R) & High-Cut Warmth (1.5 kHz to 16.0 kHz): Because the delayed voices are continuously pitch-decorrelated and rolled off in the extreme highs (High Cut), they fuse with the dry center channel to create a wide stereo halo without producing static comb-filter notches in MONO.

5.6 Automatic Multi-Mic Phase & Polarity Alignment (ALIGN ALL & Φ)#

When recording a drum kit, guitar cabinet, acoustic piano, or horn section with multiple microphones, physical distance differences between capsules create acoustic arrival-time offsets. Sound travels through studio air at approximately 343 meters per second (1,125 feet per second), meaning every 34.3 cm (13.5 inches) of extra distance between a close microphone and an overhead or room microphone delays the acoustic wavefront by 1.0 millisecond (1000 microseconds, or 48 samples at 48.0 kHz).

5.6.1 Why Multi-Mic Time Offsets Cause Comb Filtering & Hollow Low End#

When two microphones capture the same instrument at slightly different arrival times and are summed together on DRUM BUS or MST MASTER BUS:

  • Frequencies whose half-wavelength matches the time offset arrive 180° out of phase and cancel each other out, carving deep, hollow notches across the frequency spectrum (known as comb filtering).
  • A snare drum captured by both 02SnrTp (close dynamic mic) and 03OvrhdL (condenser mic 63 cm / 25 inches above the snare) suffers a 1.85 ms (1850 us) arrival delay at the overhead capsule. Without phase alignment, summing the overheads with the close snare mic thins out the 200 Hz snare body and smears the initial stick transient into a double flam.
  • Similarly, a snare bottom microphone pointed upward at the snare wires moves in the opposite mechanical direction from the top snare microphone, requiring a 180° polarity inversion (Ø) in addition to time alignment.

5.6.3 Why Delay Alone Cannot Fix Ported Cabinets, Bass DI + Amp, or Dispersive Drum Kits#

Sub-sample time-of-flight delay (Φ) and 180° polarity inversion (Ø) solve pure physical distance offsets through air. However, in real rehearsal rooms and tracking studios, multi-mic sources also suffer from frequency-dependent phase dispersion (non-linear group delay):

  • Bass DI + Miked Ported Bass Cabinet (BassDi + BassAmp): A tuned bass-reflex cabinet port acts as a high-order acoustic high-pass resonator around 45 Hz to 85 Hz, rotating the phase of the low E and A strings by 90° to 180° relative to the upper harmonics at 400 Hz. Even after you align the initial pick transient in time (Φ), the 60 Hz fundamental from the cabinet mic can sit 140° out of phase with the DI box, stealing 6 dB to 12 dB of sub-bass weight when both faders are up.
  • Multi-Mic Drum Kits & Dual-Mic Guitar Cabinets: Drum shell resonances, off-axis cardioid proximity filters, and loudspeaker crossover networks rotate low-frequency phase differently from midrange transient phase. No single time delay or 180° polarity switch can bring both 65 Hz kick chest thump and 2.5 kHz beater attack into 0° constructive alignment simultaneously.

  • Clicking the PDC pill in the Top Deck opens the Latency & PDC Transparency Breakdown popover (shown in Figure 5.4 above), which reports every latency stage in your studio system in both Samples (smp) and Milliseconds (ms):

5.7.6 Low-Latency Monitoring During Overdubs#

When you are late in a mix and have inserted a high-latency lookahead mastering plugin or linear-phase EQ (.clap or macOS Audio Unit) on a track or Subgroup Bus, recording a last-minute vocal or guitar overdub through that high-latency plugin would introduce a distracting echo in the musician's headphones.

Clicking Low-Latency Monitoring in the Top Deck solves this immediately:

  • Cave Recorder temporarily bypasses high-latency CLAP and Audio Unit plugins only on record-armed (R) live monitoring paths and their downstream buses, dropping the musician's headphone monitoring latency back down to pure zero-latency built-in console processing (0 smp graph PDC).
  • All unarmed playback tracks continue playing with full Plugin Delay Compensation.
  • As soon as you finish recording the Overdub Pass and disarm the track (or toggle Low-Latency Monitoring off in the Top Deck), the high-latency plugins re-engage automatically without losing a single plugin parameter setting.

5.8 Built-In Non-Destructive Audio Restoration (ADC De-Clipper & 12-Harmonic Hum Canceller)#

In a high-energy rehearsal or live tracking session, a drummer will occasionally hit a rimshot 6 dB harder than during soundcheck and flat-top the hardware preamp's analog-to-digital converter (0.0 dBFS), or a vintage tube combo amp will pick up 50 Hz or 60 Hz power-line buzz in an older basement venue.

Instead of forcing you to export stems to an external audio restoration editor, Cave Recorder includes two non-destructive studio restoration tools built directly into the console and timeline:

  1. [REPAIR CLIPPING] (Two-Tier C² Quintic Hermite Spline & 16th-Order Burg Autoregressive De-Clipper):
    • Automatically detects contiguous flat-topped digital saturation bursts where the waveform hits the converter ceiling (default -0.1 dBFS threshold, user-adjustable from -12.0 dBFS to 0.0 dBFS for hard-clipped external preamps).
    • Short Clips (1 to 8 samples): Reconstructs the missing transient peak using C²-Continuous Quintic Hermite Spline Interpolation anchored to the unclipped amplitude, first-derivative slope velocity, and second-derivative curvature immediately preceding and following the clip burst.
    • Medium & Long Clips (9 to 256 samples): Fits a 16th-Order Forward/Backward Burg Autoregressive (AR) Linear Prediction Model to the 512 clean context samples surrounding the clipped plateau, predicts the natural harmonic waveform trajectory forward and backward across the gap with raised-cosine crossfade blending, and restores the lost 3 dB to 6 dB transient dome cleanly.
  2. 50 Hz / 60 Hz 12-Harmonic Comb Hum Canceller (HumCanceller):
    • Power-line ground loops and neon/dimmer transformer buzz never sit solely at 50 Hz or 60 Hz. They generate a sharp comb of odd and even harmonics (60, 120, 180, 240, 300, 360, 420, 480, 540, 600, 660, 720 Hz) that sounds like an angry hornet nest inside guitar and bass tracks. Trying to cut all 12 harmonics with regular EQ bands wastes your entire parametric equalizer and hollows out the low-mid tone of the instrument.
    • The dedicated HumCanceller cascades 12 ultra-narrow constant-Q biquad notch filters (Q = 20.0 to 100.0, default Q = 60.0, bandwidth < 1.0 Hz at the fundamental) locked to f_0, 2f_0, ..., 12f_0 (50.0 Hz European or 60.0 Hz North American mains, with fundamental fine-tuning from 45.0 Hz to 65.0 Hz and selectable Odd Harmonics Only vs. Odd + Even Harmonics). Because each notch is less than a couple of Hertz wide, 50/60 Hz buzz vanishes completely while 99% of the surrounding bass and guitar note warmth passes through untouched.
